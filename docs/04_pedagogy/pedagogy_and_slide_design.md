# Pedagogy & Slide Design System

**Status:** Canonical  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Project:** Invest Smarter with AI  
**Domain Authority:** Teaching method, lesson structure, slide design, exercises, and assessment delivery. Canonical rubric dimensions, scoring, and completion thresholds belong to [Templates & Rubrics](../03_templates/templates_and_rubrics.md).

---

# 1. Purpose

This document defines **how the investment curriculum should be taught**.

It does not redefine:

- the project vision;
- the investment methodology;
- course scope;
- research-source standards;
- compliance rules.

Those belong to their respective canonical documents: [Source of Truth](../00_project/source_of_truth.md), [Methodology](../02_methodology/investment_methodology.md), [curriculum](../01_curriculum/), [Research Standards](../05_research/data_and_research_standards.md), and [Compliance](../06_compliance/legal_risk_compliance.md).

The teaching system is designed for busy professionals and entrepreneurs. Therefore, every lesson must maximize:

**clarity + practical usefulness + retention + decision quality per minute.**

---

# 2. Teaching North Star

The program should not make students feel that investing requires becoming a full-time analyst.

It should make them capable of saying:

> I understand the decision, I know what matters, I know what to ignore, I know how AI can accelerate the work, and I know what I must verify myself.

---

# 3. Pedagogical Model

Use the following default sequence:

**Problem → Concept → Framework → Example → AI Workflow → Verification → Decision → Action → Deliverable**

Not every lesson needs a separate slide for every step, but the logical progression should remain.

---

# 4. Start With the Decision

Do not begin with definitions unless they are necessary.

Prefer:

> Should I invest this cash or keep it liquid?

over:

> Definition of liquidity.

Prefer:

> Is this stock cheap or is the business deteriorating?

over:

> Definition of P/E ratio.

The student should know why a concept matters before learning its terminology.

---

# 5. Progressive Depth

Teach in three layers.

## Layer 1 — Must Know

The minimum needed to make a reasonable decision.

## Layer 2 — Useful to Know

Adds context or improves judgment.

## Layer 3 — Only When Needed

Sector-, company-, or situation-specific depth.

Do not teach Layer 3 simply because it exists.

---

# 6. Course Design Standard

Each course should contain:

1. Course promise
2. Learning outcomes
3. Core mental model
4. Lessons / modules
5. Realistic examples
6. AI workflows embedded in relevant lessons
7. Verification rules
8. Common mistakes
9. Final practical deliverable
10. Graduation test

The final deliverable is mandatory.

A course is not complete because the student consumed the content.

A course is complete when the student can perform the target skill.

---

# 7. Lesson Design Standard

A lesson should answer four questions:

## Why?
Why does this matter?

## What?
What concept must be understood?

## How?
What repeatable framework should be used?

## Now what?
What decision or action can the student now make?

---

# 8. AI Within the Lesson

AI is embedded throughout the program.

Do not create artificial "AI sections" when AI does not add value.

Use AI when it can materially reduce:

- reading time;
- comparison time;
- extraction work;
- information organization;
- first-pass research;
- repetitive monitoring.

Use the canonical [AI Research Prompt Template](../03_templates/templates_and_rubrics.md#18-universal-ai-research-prompt-template) and [Research verification requirements](../05_research/data_and_research_standards.md#24-ai-hallucination-control). The following points guide teaching the workflow, not a separate prompt schema:

- context;
- source boundaries;
- task;
- output format;
- constraints;
- verification step;
- human decision.

---

# 9. AI Demonstration Pattern

When teaching an AI workflow, show:

## Input
What material is provided?

Example:

- annual report;
- earnings release;
- ETF factsheet;
- broker pricing page.

## Prompt
What is asked?

## Output
What useful structure should AI produce?

## Verification
What must be checked manually?

## Decision
What remains the student's responsibility?

This is more valuable than teaching isolated prompt tricks.

# 10. Slide Design Principles

## One Main Idea per Slide

A slide should have one dominant message.

Avoid using slides as textbook pages.

---

## Headline Should State the Insight

Weak:

> P/E Ratio

Better:

> A low P/E can mean opportunity — or deterioration

Weak:

> Supply Chain

Better:

> Map the value chain before you judge the company's economics

---

## Prefer Visual Explanation

Use:

- diagrams;
- timelines;
- process flows;
- decision trees;
- checklists;
- scorecards;
- comparison tables;
- industry maps;
- value-chain maps;
- annotated financial charts;
- before/after cases.

---

## Use Text Sparingly

Slides should support explanation, not replace it.

Long explanations belong in:

- speaker notes;
- course notes;
- companion materials;
- source documents.

---

# 11. Recommended Slide Types

Use a small reusable system.

## Type A — Decision Slide
Frames the problem.

## Type B — Concept Slide
Explains one essential concept.

## Type C — Framework Slide
Shows a repeatable decision process.

## Type D — Example Slide
Applies the concept to a realistic situation.

## Type E — Comparison Slide
Shows alternatives and trade-offs.

## Type F — Business Map
Shows how a company or industry works.

## Type G — Financial Chart
Shows trend, valuation, margin, cash flow, or price context.

## Type H — AI Workflow
Shows input → prompt → output → verification.

## Type I — Mistake / Red Flag
Shows what commonly goes wrong.

## Type J — Action / Deliverable
Tells the student what to execute.

---

# 12. Financial Examples

Examples should be realistic and current enough to feel relevant, but the lesson should not depend on one company's exact current price.

When possible:

- use real companies;
- clearly date market data;
- separate facts from hypothetical assumptions;
- avoid implying future returns;
- avoid cherry-picked examples that make the framework appear infallible.

When materially relevant, investment examples should address what could go wrong, downside, time horizon, concentration, and thesis invalidation. This rule guides how examples are taught; canonical investment-risk definitions follow [Methodology's Risk Framework](../02_methodology/investment_methodology.md#13-risk-framework).

---

# 13. Use of Case Studies

Case studies are preferred when they illustrate:

- business-model understanding;
- an industry cycle;
- a supply-chain bottleneck;
- valuation compression;
- a genuine dislocation;
- a value trap;
- a thesis break;
- a buy / hold / trim / sell decision.

The purpose is to teach the framework, not celebrate the outcome.

---

# 14. Numbers and Math

The target audience does not need unnecessary mathematical complexity.

Use calculations when they improve a decision.

Examples:

- real return;
- CAGR;
- valuation multiple;
- FCF yield;
- position weight;
- drawdown;
- scenario valuation.

Explain the intuition first.

Formula second.

---

# 15. Terminology

Use standard financial English when it is the language used by markets and source documents.

Example:

**Free Cash Flow (FCF)** — explain cash generation intuitively, using the canonical [Research definition](../05_research/data_and_research_standards.md#11-free-cash-flow).

Do not create awkward translations that make primary-source research harder.

The course may be delivered in Spanish while retaining standard market terminology.

---

# 16. Exercises

Exercises should require judgment, not memorization.

Prefer:

- classify;
- compare;
- explain;
- map;
- diagnose;
- decide;
- prioritize.

Avoid excessive questions such as:

> What is the definition of EBITDA?

Prefer:

> Why might EV/EBITDA make a highly capital-intensive company look cheaper than it really is?

---

# 17. Quizzes

Use quizzes when they test core misconceptions.

Good quiz topics:

- ETF ≠ asset class;
- price ≠ value;
- cheap ≠ undervalued;
- volatility ≠ permanent loss;
- growth ≠ quality;
- stock decline ≠ opportunity;
- AI answer ≠ verified fact.

Quizzes should be short.

They are checkpoints, not the core learning experience.

---

# 18. Deliverables

Every course ends with a practical artifact.

Canonical examples:

- Investor Profile;
- Investment System;
- Rapid Business Map;
- Valuation & Expectations Card;
- Opportunity Card;
- Portfolio Operating System.

Curriculum specifies the required course artifact. [Templates & Rubrics](../03_templates/templates_and_rubrics.md) owns its fields and assessment criteria. Deliverables should be reusable after the course.

The student should gradually build a personal investment operating system.

---

# 19. Assessment and Feedback Delivery

Use the [Universal Evaluation Rubric](../03_templates/templates_and_rubrics.md#5-universal-evaluation-rubric), relevant artifact rubric, and [course graduation rubric](../03_templates/templates_and_rubrics.md#23-graduation-rubric-by-course). Templates owns the dimensions, scoring, and completion threshold.

During feedback, ask the student to explain the decision, show supporting evidence, acknowledge uncertainty, and identify risk. Connect feedback to the applicable rubric and help the student resolve the material gaps.

Do not grade based on whether the stock later went up or down.

---

# 20. Graduation Principle

Judge process, not hindsight.

A good decision can have a bad outcome.

A bad decision can have a good outcome.

The course should reinforce:

> Process quality is controllable. Market outcomes are not.

---

# 21. Time-Efficient Teaching

The student should leave every lesson with a shortcut that improves process quality.

Examples:

- a checklist;
- an AI prompt;
- a decision tree;
- a source hierarchy;
- a template;
- a monitoring rule.

Do not add content unless it earns its time cost.

---

# 22. Content Density Rule

When a slide or lesson feels overloaded:

1. remove secondary detail;
2. move optional detail to notes;
3. split the concept if both parts are essential;
4. convert lists into a visual framework;
5. ask whether the content changes a decision.

---

# 23. Speaker Notes

Speaker notes should contain:

- explanation;
- nuance;
- examples;
- transition;
- likely student questions;
- caveats;
- source references where relevant.

Slides should remain clean.

---

# 24. Course Consistency

All courses should feel like one operating system.

Reuse canonical language:

- business quality;
- expectations;
- valuation;
- dislocation;
- catalyst;
- risk;
- thesis;
- invalidation;
- Core / Satellite;
- BUY / HOLD / ADD / TRIM / SELL.

Do not invent competing terminology in different modules.

---

# 25. What to Avoid

Do not:

- teach for academic completeness;
- overload slides with text;
- turn every lesson into a finance lecture;
- add indicators because they are popular;
- show AI as magic;
- present fair value as exact;
- reward unnecessary activity;
- equate complexity with sophistication;
- create advanced modules without demonstrated demand.

---

# 26. Pedagogy North Star

Every lesson should help the student answer:

> What should I understand, what can AI accelerate, what must I verify, and what decision am I now better equipped to make?
