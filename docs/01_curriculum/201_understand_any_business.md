# 201 — Understand Any Business

## Course promise

**Learn how a company makes money, how its industry works and where it sits in the value chain.**

This course teaches rapid business understanding.

The goal is not institutional-grade due diligence.

The goal is to build a sufficiently accurate first view of a company in approximately 30–60 minutes and decide whether it deserves:

- PASS;
- WATCHLIST;
- DEEPER RESEARCH.

AI is used as a research accelerator, but the student must retain judgment and verify material facts.

---

# 1. What the student should be able to do

By the end of the course, the student should be able to:

- explain a company's business model simply;
- identify customers, products, revenue streams and cost drivers;
- map the industry;
- map the company's value chain / supply chain;
- identify the most important competitors;
- understand the company's main economic engine;
- identify 3–5 KPIs that matter most for that industry;
- understand basic financial quality;
- identify competitive advantages and vulnerabilities;
- use filings, investor materials and AI to learn a new industry quickly.

---

# 2. The 30–60 Minute Business Understanding Framework

Use seven blocks:

1. Business model
2. Industry
3. Value chain / supply chain
4. Economics
5. Competitive position
6. Key metrics
7. Risks

## Output

The student should be able to explain the company on one page.

---

# 3. Lesson 1 — What does the company actually sell?

Ask:

- What product or service is sold?
- Who pays?
- Why do customers buy?
- How frequently do customers buy?
- Is revenue recurring or transactional?
- Is pricing fixed, negotiated or market-driven?
- Which segments matter most?

## Rule

If you cannot explain the business in plain language, you do not yet understand it.

---

# 4. Lesson 2 — Revenue model

Possible models:

- unit sales;
- subscriptions;
- usage-based;
- advertising;
- marketplace take-rate;
- licensing;
- transaction fees;
- project-based;
- commodity-linked;
- recurring service;
- hybrid.

## Important questions

- What drives volume?
- What drives price?
- What drives mix?
- What drives retention?
- What drives customer acquisition?

---

# 5. Lesson 3 — Customer analysis

Understand:

- customer type;
- customer concentration;
- contract length;
- switching costs;
- cyclicality;
- customer bargaining power.

## Useful classification

### B2C
Consumer-driven.

### B2B
Business customers.

### B2G
Government or regulated procurement.

### Platform
Multiple-sided marketplace.

## Risk

A company can grow while becoming more dependent on a small number of customers.

---

# 6. Lesson 4 — Industry map

The student should learn an unfamiliar industry quickly.

## Build a simple map

- upstream suppliers;
- core producers;
- distributors;
- channels;
- customers;
- substitutes;
- regulators;
- complementary products.

## Questions

- How large is the market?
- Is the industry growing?
- Is it cyclical?
- Is it concentrated?
- Who has pricing power?
- Where are margins highest?
- What are the barriers to entry?

---

# 7. Lesson 5 — Supply chain / value chain mapping

This is a core skill.

## Objective

Understand where value is created and where risk accumulates.

## Map

**Inputs → Production → Distribution → Customer**

Then identify:

- critical suppliers;
- bottlenecks;
- geographic dependencies;
- commodity exposure;
- logistics risk;
- manufacturing constraints;
- inventory dynamics;
- customer concentration.

## Why it matters

Many investment opportunities and risks come from supply-chain changes before they appear clearly in headline earnings.

# 9. Lesson 6 — Business economics

Focus on the few numbers that reveal the economic engine.

## Revenue

Is the company growing?

## Gross margin

What is left after direct cost of producing the product/service?

Can indicate:

- pricing power;
- product mix;
- production efficiency.

## Operating margin

How efficiently does the company convert gross profit into operating profit?

## Free Cash Flow

Does accounting profit become cash?

## CapEx

How much capital must be reinvested to maintain or grow the business?

## Debt

How dependent is the business on financing?

## Share count

Is the company diluting shareholders?

## ROIC

When useful, ask:

> Does the company create attractive returns on the capital it reinvests?

---

# 10. Lesson 7 — Quality of growth

Not all growth is equal.

Ask:

- Is growth organic?
- Is growth acquisition-driven?
- Are margins improving or deteriorating?
- Is FCF growing?
- Is share count growing rapidly?
- Is debt funding the growth?
- Is growth dependent on one customer/product/geography?

## Better question

Not only:

> How fast is revenue growing?

But:

> What is required to produce that growth?

---

# 11. Lesson 8 — Competitive advantage

Potential sources:

- brand;
- cost advantage;
- scale;
- network effects;
- switching costs;
- intellectual property;
- regulation;
- distribution;
- ecosystem;
- data;
- customer trust.

## Test

A moat should have an economic consequence.

Examples:

- better margins;
- higher retention;
- lower acquisition cost;
- better pricing;
- higher returns on capital.

If the advantage has no visible economic effect, be skeptical.

---

# 12. Lesson 9 — Competitive vulnerability

Ask:

- What could make the product irrelevant?
- Could a customer switch easily?
- Could a new entrant undercut price?
- Is technology changing the industry?
- Is the company dependent on one supplier?
- Is the company dependent on one platform?
- Is regulation a material risk?

# 14. Lesson 11 — Source hierarchy

For rapid company research, start with primary material.

Preferred order:

1. annual report / 10-K;
2. quarterly report / 10-Q;
3. investor presentation;
4. earnings release;
5. earnings call / transcript;
6. company website;
7. industry sources;
8. reputable media;
9. secondary aggregators;
10. social media as idea-generation only.

# 15. AI workflow — Understand the business

## Inputs

Ideally provide:

- latest annual report;
- latest quarterly report;
- investor presentation;
- earnings release.

## Prompt

> Act as a junior equity analyst. Using only the documents provided, explain:
> 1. what the company sells;
> 2. who pays;
> 3. how revenue is generated;
> 4. key segments;
> 5. key geographies;
> 6. major cost drivers;
> 7. what drives margins;
> 8. major competitors;
> 9. major dependencies;
> 10. what could structurally impair the business.
>
> Separate facts from interpretation. Cite the source document for every material conclusion. Do not recommend the stock.

---

# 16. AI workflow — Learn the industry fast

## Prompt

> Assume I need to understand this industry quickly before evaluating an investment. Teach me the 20% of concepts that explain 80% of the economics. Cover:
> - value chain;
> - major players;
> - how companies make money;
> - industry cycle;
> - key KPIs;
> - pricing power;
> - bottlenecks;
> - regulation;
> - major risks;
> - vocabulary I need to understand earnings calls.
>
> Finish with 10 questions I should investigate before investing.

---

# 17. AI workflow — Map the supply chain

## Prompt

> Build a value-chain map for this company and industry:
> inputs → suppliers → production → distribution → end customer.
>
> For each stage identify:
> - critical players;
> - bargaining power;
> - bottlenecks;
> - geographic concentration;
> - commodity exposure;
> - switching costs;
> - major risks.
>
> Clearly mark any point that is inferred rather than confirmed by a source.

---

# 18. AI workflow — Find the numbers that matter

## Prompt

> From the attached filings, identify the 5 financial or operating metrics that best explain this company's economics. Explain why each matters, how it has changed over time and what would be a warning sign.

---

# 19. Human verification

The investor must still answer:

- Does this business make intuitive economic sense?
- Are the AI-selected KPIs really the right ones?
- Are critical numbers sourced correctly?
- Is the industry map complete enough?
- What does management emphasize?
- What do competitors say about the same industry?

---

# 20. Common mistakes

- reading financial statements before understanding the business;
- confusing revenue growth with business quality;
- ignoring customer concentration;
- ignoring the supply chain;
- copying a generic moat checklist;
- treating all sectors with the same metrics;
- letting AI summarize without checking source documents;
- spending hours on details before deciding whether the company is worth researching.

---

# 22. Graduation test

The student passes when they can explain a new company to another person without reading from a report and answer:

- How does it make money?
- What drives growth?
- What drives margins?
- What does the industry depend on?
- Where is the company in the value chain?
- What 3–5 metrics matter most?
- What could break the business?

If these answers are unclear, valuation should not begin yet.
