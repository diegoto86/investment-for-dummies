# 401 — Spot the Opportunity

This course applies the audience, scope, and six-course architecture in the [Source of Truth](../00_project/source_of_truth.md). Analytical principles follow [Investment Methodology](../02_methodology/investment_methodology.md); sourcing and verification follow [Research Standards](../05_research/data_and_research_standards.md). [Templates & Rubrics](../03_templates/templates_and_rubrics.md) owns form schemas and completion thresholds.

## Course promise

**Find dislocations, catalysts and attractive entry points without becoming a full-time trader.**

This course turns business understanding and valuation into actionable opportunity detection.

The student learns to identify when:

- the market may be overreacting;
- a high-quality business becomes attractively priced;
- a temporary problem creates a dislocation;
- a catalyst may change expectations;
- technical context improves entry quality.

The goal is not short-term trading.

The goal is intelligent timing for medium- to long-term investing.

---

# 1. What the student should be able to do

By the end of the course, the student should be able to:

- define a market dislocation;
- identify why a stock moved;
- separate temporary problems from structural impairment;
- identify catalysts;
- distinguish opportunity from value trap;
- understand basic market and sector context;
- use a minimal technical framework;
- define an entry zone;
- size a position;
- define thesis invalidation;
- use AI to investigate events quickly.

---

# 2. Core framework

## Quality + Mispricing + Catalyst + Setup

A strong opportunity often combines:

### Quality
A business worth owning.

### Mispricing
Price appears disconnected from reasonable value.

### Catalyst
A reason expectations may change.

### Setup
A sufficiently attractive entry context.

These are analytical dimensions, not mandatory gates, under [Methodology](../02_methodology/investment_methodology.md#22-good-company--good-investment). A catalyst is optional and a favorable technical setup is not required for a valid thesis.

---

# 3. Lesson 1 — What is a dislocation?

A dislocation is a meaningful divergence between:

- price movement;
- underlying economic change.

Examples:

- earnings miss;
- temporary margin pressure;
- regulatory headline;
- sector selloff;
- forced selling;
- inventory correction;
- one-off operational issue;
- macro shock.

## Core question

> Did the price fall more than the value?

---

# 4. Lesson 2 — Temporary vs structural

## Temporary

Examples:

- short-term inventory correction;
- delayed order;
- temporary input-cost spike;
- one-off charge;
- short-term FX effect.

## Structural

Examples:

- product obsolescence;
- permanent market-share loss;
- broken balance sheet;
- major regulatory impairment;
- customer migration;
- loss of competitive advantage.

## Rule

A dislocation is attractive only if the economic damage is smaller than the price reaction.

---

# 5. Lesson 3 — Catalyst

A catalyst is useful but not mandatory. A strong analysis may conclude: "No identifiable catalyst is required." Do not invent a catalyst to satisfy the framework or rubric.

Possible catalysts:

- earnings recovery;
- margin normalization;
- inventory normalization;
- product launch;
- capacity expansion;
- new contract;
- regulatory approval;
- debt reduction;
- restructuring;
- buyback;
- cycle recovery.

## Important

A catalyst should be plausible, observable and relevant.

---

# 6. Lesson 4 — Market context

Understand enough context to avoid fighting major forces blindly.

Check:

- broad index trend;
- sector trend;
- industry cycle;
- earnings calendar;
- major macro event only when relevant.

## Avoid

Turning the course into macro forecasting.

The goal is context, not prediction.

---

# 7. Lesson 5 — Sector context

Ask:

- Is the whole sector weak?
- Is the company uniquely weak?
- Are peers reporting the same problem?
- Is the industry entering a downturn or recovery?
- Are customers changing behavior?

## High-value action

Compare the company's issue with:

- direct competitors;
- customers;
- suppliers.

This often reveals whether the problem is company-specific or industry-wide.

---

# 8. Lesson 6 — Technical minimum viable framework

Use technical analysis only as a secondary timing and risk-context tool under [Methodology](../02_methodology/investment_methodology.md#10-technical-context). A favorable technical setup is not required for a valid investment thesis.

## Trend

- rising;
- falling;
- sideways.

## Support / resistance

Identify areas where price previously reacted.

## Volume

Can help assess whether a move has unusual participation.

## 50-day moving average (50DMA)

Intermediate trend reference.

## 200-day moving average (200DMA)

Longer-term trend reference.

## RSI

Used only as contextual evidence of stretched conditions.

## Rule

Technical indicators do not replace the investment thesis.

---

# 9. Lesson 7 — Priority setups

## Pullback in an uptrend

Strong business, intact trend, temporary weakness.

## 200DMA opportunity

High-quality company near a long-term technical reference after a pullback.

## Constructive base

Price consolidates while fundamentals remain intact.

## Post-earnings dislocation

Large move after results where market reaction may exceed fundamental change.

---

# 10. Lesson 8 — Entry zone

Avoid pretending one exact price is perfect.

Define:

- ideal zone;
- acceptable zone;
- no-chase level.

## Why zones are better

Markets are noisy.

Exact prices create false precision.

---

# 11. Lesson 9 — Scaling in

Instead of committing all capital immediately:

- initial position;
- add if thesis confirms;
- add at better valuation if thesis remains intact.

## Warning

Scaling in is not automatically averaging down.

The thesis must remain valid.

---

# 12. Lesson 10 — Position sizing

Sizing depends on:

- conviction;
- downside;
- uncertainty;
- liquidity;
- correlation;
- existing portfolio exposure.

## Practical rule

The worse the uncertainty, the smaller the initial position.

---

# 13. Lesson 11 — Invalidation

Every opportunity should answer:

> What would prove me wrong?

Possible invalidation:

- customer loss;
- guidance collapse;
- debt issue;
- competitive deterioration;
- permanent margin damage;
- thesis catalyst disappears.

## Important

Price alone is not always thesis invalidation.

---

# 14. Lesson 12 — Research dispositions

Use [Methodology's research dispositions](../02_methodology/investment_methodology.md#stage-8--decide): PASS, WATCH, RESEARCH MORE, and BUY CANDIDATE. The Opportunity Card uses the dispositions relevant to this stage. BUY CANDIDATE is a research conclusion, not an executed BUY action.

## BUY CANDIDATE

Business understood, valuation attractive, risks acceptable.

## WATCH

Interesting, but entry or evidence is insufficient.

## PASS

Risk, valuation or business quality does not justify further attention.

# 15. AI workflow — What happened?

## Prompt

> This stock moved approximately X% after [event]. Investigate the move using the sources I provide and separate:
> 1. confirmed facts;
> 2. management commentary;
> 3. market interpretation;
> 4. what changed in the investment thesis;
> 5. temporary factors;
> 6. potentially structural factors;
> 7. unanswered questions.
>
> Do not assume the market is wrong.

---

# 16. AI workflow — Compare with peers

## Prompt

> Compare this company's latest issue with its three closest peers. Determine whether the weakness appears:
> - company-specific;
> - industry-wide;
> - macro-driven.
>
> Show the evidence for each conclusion and identify contradictions.

---

# 17. AI workflow — Catalyst map

## Prompt

> Based on the company materials and current situation, build a catalyst map for the next 6–24 months. For each catalyst include:
> - what must happen;
> - observable evidence;
> - likely timing;
> - what could prevent it;
> - whether it affects revenue, margin, cash flow, valuation or sentiment.

---

# 18. AI workflow — Red team the opportunity

## Prompt

> Assume my thesis is wrong. Build the strongest bear case against this investment. Focus on:
> - structural deterioration;
> - competitive risks;
> - balance-sheet risk;
> - customer/supplier risk;
> - valuation risk;
> - reasons the dislocation may be justified.
>
> Do not repeat my bull case.

---

# 19. Human decision

Before investing, answer:

- Do I understand the business?
- Is valuation attractive?
- What caused the dislocation?
- Is the issue temporary or structural?
- Is a catalyst useful, or is no identifiable catalyst required?
- What would invalidate the thesis?
- How much capital should I risk?

---

# 20. Common mistakes

- buying because a stock fell;
- assuming oversold means undervalued;
- overusing technical indicators;
- chasing the first rebound;
- ignoring the sector cycle;
- assuming a catalyst without evidence;
- averaging down automatically;
- using AI to confirm the thesis instead of challenge it.

Assess catalysts when useful; technical fields apply only when they improve the decision. Templates owns the fields.

# 22. Graduation test

Apply the [Opportunity Card rubric](../03_templates/templates_and_rubrics.md#opportunity-card-rubric), [Course 401 graduation rubric](../03_templates/templates_and_rubrics.md#401--spot-the-opportunity), and [canonical scoring threshold](../03_templates/templates_and_rubrics.md#5-universal-evaluation-rubric). A justified no-catalyst conclusion is acceptable.

The student passes when they can explain:

- why the stock is down or mispriced;
- why the issue may be temporary;
- why the business remains worth owning;
- what could close the valuation gap, or why no identifiable catalyst is required;
- what would prove the thesis wrong;
- how much capital should be committed.

If the answer is simply "the stock is cheap now," the analysis is incomplete.
