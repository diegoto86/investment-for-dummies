# 000 — Investing Foundations

This course applies the audience, scope, and six-course architecture in the [Source of Truth](../00_project/source_of_truth.md). Analytical principles follow [Investment Methodology](../02_methodology/investment_methodology.md); sourcing and verification follow [Research Standards](../05_research/data_and_research_standards.md). [Templates & Rubrics](../03_templates/templates_and_rubrics.md) owns form schemas and completion thresholds.

## Course promise

**Understand money, markets and what you are actually buying.**

This course gives the student the minimum conceptual foundation needed to invest with judgment, avoid basic category mistakes, and use AI as a learning accelerator without outsourcing decisions.

The objective is not to memorize financial vocabulary. The objective is to understand the few concepts that determine most investment decisions.

---

# 1. What the student should be able to do

By the end of the course, the student should be able to:

- explain the difference between saving, investing and speculating;
- understand inflation, compounding, risk, return, liquidity and time horizon;
- distinguish an asset from an investment vehicle;
- understand the basic role of cash, deposits, bonds, stocks, ETFs, real estate, commodities, crypto and private markets;
- understand how brokers and exchanges fit into the system;
- explain why price and value are not the same;
- identify the main source of return and main risk of an investment;
- define a first Investor Profile;
- use ChatGPT or Claude to learn financial concepts faster while verifying critical information.

# 2. Core mental model

## Return — Risk — Time

Every investment decision is a trade-off between:

- expected return;
- risk of loss;
- liquidity;
- time.

There is no asset that maximizes all four simultaneously.

### Key question

> What am I being paid for, and what risk am I taking in exchange?

---

# 3. Lesson 1 — Saving vs investing

## Must know

### Saving
Capital reserved primarily for:

- emergencies;
- near-term spending;
- liquidity;
- capital preservation.

### Investing
Capital committed with the expectation of:

- income;
- appreciation;
- compounding;
- long-term wealth creation.

### Speculating
Taking a position mainly because of an expected price move rather than because of durable economics or a defined investment thesis.

## Decision

The student should classify money into:

- money that must stay liquid;
- money that can be invested;
- money that can tolerate volatility.

## Common mistake

Investing money that may be needed soon.

---

# 4. Lesson 2 — Inflation and purchasing power

## Concepts

- nominal return;
- real return;
- purchasing power;
- inflation drag.

### Example

If an investment returns 5% and inflation is 3%, the real gain is approximately 2% before tax and fees.

## Decision

The student should understand why "not losing money nominally" is not the same as preserving purchasing power.

---

# 5. Lesson 3 — Compounding

## Concepts

- principal;
- return;
- reinvestment;
- time;
- contribution rate.

## Main idea

Compounding is powerful because returns begin generating additional returns.

## Important nuance

For most working professionals, **savings rate + time + consistency** matter more initially than finding extraordinary investments.

## Exercise

Compare:

- investing €500/month for 20 years;
- investing €1,000/month for 10 years;
- investing a lump sum and never adding more capital.

The goal is not prediction. The goal is to understand the effect of time and contributions.

---

# 6. Lesson 4 — Risk

## Must know

### Volatility
How much price moves.

### Drawdown
Decline from a previous peak.

### Permanent loss of capital
When the economic value of the investment is impaired or capital cannot reasonably recover.

### Liquidity risk
Difficulty exiting at a reasonable price.

### Credit risk
Risk that a borrower cannot repay.

### Business risk
Risk that a company loses earnings power.

### Valuation risk
Paying too much even for a good asset.

## Principle

**Volatility is visible. Permanent capital loss is more important.**

# 7. Lesson 5 — The investment universe

The purpose is context, not mastery.

## Cash and banking products

- checking accounts;
- remunerated accounts;
- term deposits;
- money-market instruments.

### What drives return?
Interest rates.

### Main risks
Inflation, bank/product-specific risk, opportunity cost.

---

## Bonds

- sovereign bonds;
- corporate bonds;
- principal;
- coupon;
- maturity;
- yield.

### What drives return?
Interest payments + price changes.

### Main risks
Interest-rate risk, inflation, credit risk.

---

## Stocks

A stock represents ownership in a company.

### Sources of return

- earnings growth;
- valuation change;
- dividends;
- buybacks.

### Main risks

- business deterioration;
- excessive valuation;
- leverage;
- dilution;
- disruption.

---

## Real estate and REITs

### Sources of return

- rent;
- asset appreciation;
- leverage.

### Main risks

- interest rates;
- occupancy;
- financing;
- local market conditions;
- illiquidity.

---

## Gold and commodities

### Role

Can act as:

- inflation hedge in some environments;
- diversification;
- cyclical exposure.

### Main limitation

Many commodities do not generate cash flows directly.

---

## Cryptoassets

Context only.

### Understand

- technological risk;
- regulatory risk;
- custody;
- extreme volatility;
- lack of conventional cash-flow valuation for many assets.

---

## Private markets

- private equity;
- venture capital;
- private credit.

### Key difference

Lower liquidity and less frequent price discovery.

# 8. Lesson 6 — Asset vs vehicle

## Asset
What ultimately generates or represents economic exposure.

Examples:

- stock;
- bond;
- gold;
- real estate.

## Vehicle
How the exposure is packaged.

Examples:

- ETF;
- mutual fund;
- investment trust.

## Critical idea

> An ETF is not an asset class.

An ETF can hold:

- stocks;
- bonds;
- commodities;
- sectors;
- countries;
- factors.

---

# 9. Lesson 7 — Three ways to invest

## Delegate

Examples:

- managed funds;
- robo-advisors;
- wealth managers.

### Advantage
Low time requirement.

### Trade-off
Less control and potentially higher cost.

---

## Index

Examples:

- broad-market ETFs;
- index funds.

### Advantage
Simple diversification and low maintenance.

### Trade-off
You own the market, including companies you would not select individually.

---

## Select

Examples:

- individual stocks;
- sectors;
- special situations.

### Advantage
Potential to exploit mispricing and concentrate in higher-conviction ideas.

### Trade-off
Requires more judgment and monitoring.

---

# 10. Lesson 8 — How markets work

## Basic actors

- company / issuer;
- exchange;
- broker;
- investor;
- market maker;
- regulator.

## Must know

- ticker;
- bid;
- ask;
- spread;
- liquidity;
- market order;
- limit order.

## Broker ≠ exchange

The broker gives the investor access to a market. The exchange is the venue where securities trade.

---

# 11. Lesson 9 — Price vs value

## Price
What the market currently asks or offers.

## Value
What the asset may reasonably be worth based on economics and future cash-generation ability.

## Core idea

> Markets continuously set prices. They do not continuously guarantee fair value.

This concept becomes central later when studying undervaluation and dislocations.

# 12. AI workflow — Learn faster, not blindly

AI is introduced from the first course as a productivity tool.

## Use AI to

- explain unfamiliar concepts;
- compare instruments;
- translate financial language;
- generate examples;
- identify what you do not understand;
- create questions for further research.

## Do not use AI to

- blindly decide what to buy;
- invent missing data;
- treat a model answer as a primary source;
- ignore date and source freshness.

## Prompt framework

### Context
What are you trying to understand?

### Task
What question should AI answer?

### Format
How should it structure the answer?

### Constraints
What should it avoid?

### Verification
What must be checked elsewhere?

## Example prompt

> I am learning the difference between stocks, bonds and broad-market ETFs. Explain how each can generate returns, their main risks, liquidity, typical time horizon and what could cause permanent capital loss. Use a comparison table. Do not recommend a specific product. Finish with five questions I should be able to answer before moving on.

# 14. Common mistakes

- investing emergency savings;
- assuming higher return means "better";
- confusing volatility with permanent loss;
- thinking ETF means low risk;
- buying something because it recently rose;
- assuming a well-known company is automatically a good investment;
- treating a broker app as an investment strategy;
- using AI as an oracle.

# 15. Deliverable — Investor Profile

Risk capacity is the financial ability to absorb losses without compromising obligations, liquidity needs, or financial stability. Risk tolerance is the psychological and emotional ability to tolerate volatility and drawdowns. Being comfortable with losses does not mean you can afford them financially.

Create a concise Investor Profile using the canonical [Template 000](../03_templates/templates_and_rubrics.md#6-template-000--investor-profile). Explain your financial objective, horizon, liquidity, capacity and tolerance for risk, capital, contributions, time budget, and preferred investment mode.

Use the form to make explicit which money you will not invest. The template owns the fields; this course teaches how to reason about them.

# 16. Graduation test

Apply the [Investor Profile completion rubric](../03_templates/templates_and_rubrics.md#completion-rubric--investor-profile) and [canonical scoring threshold](../03_templates/templates_and_rubrics.md#5-universal-evaluation-rubric). The questions below help demonstrate the course outcomes.

The student passes this level if they can clearly explain:

- what they are buying;
- how it can make money;
- how it can lose money;
- when they may need the capital;
- why investing is appropriate for that money.

If those answers are unclear, the student should not move to execution.
