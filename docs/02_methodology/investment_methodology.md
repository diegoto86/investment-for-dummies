# Investment Methodology

**Status:** Canonical  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Project:** Invest Smarter with AI  
**Purpose:** Define the investment philosophy, decision framework, portfolio logic, and analytical use of AI that must remain consistent across the entire program.

---

# 1. Methodology Purpose

This document defines **how we think as investors**.

The curriculum explains what students learn in each course.  
This methodology defines the common investment logic used across those courses.

The [Source of Truth](../00_project/source_of_truth.md) governs audience, program scope, and curriculum architecture. [Research Standards](../05_research/data_and_research_standards.md) governs operational sourcing, citations, freshness, data verification, and AI research verification. [Templates & Rubrics](../03_templates/templates_and_rubrics.md) governs forms and assessment criteria.

The target student is not a professional equity analyst or full-time trader.

The target student is a busy professional, executive, specialist, or entrepreneur who wants to:

- invest over the medium to long term;
- build wealth systematically;
- understand businesses efficiently;
- learn unfamiliar industries quickly;
- identify undervaluation and market dislocations;
- recognize attractive entry and exit opportunities;
- manage risk without watching markets all day;
- use AI to compress research time without outsourcing judgment.

The methodology must optimize for:

**decision quality per unit of time.**

# 2. Core Philosophy

## 2.1 We invest, we do not constantly trade

The default horizon is medium to long term.

The methodology is designed around:

- business ownership;
- durable economics;
- valuation;
- expectations;
- catalysts;
- risk;
- portfolio construction.

Short-term price action may improve entry or exit decisions, but it is not the primary source of conviction.

---

## 2.2 Good company ≠ good investment

A high-quality company can be a poor investment if expectations are already too optimistic.

A mediocre company can occasionally be mispriced, but low valuation alone is not enough.

The investment decision considers these analytical dimensions:

**Business Quality + Expectations + Valuation + Dislocation + Catalyst + Risk**

These dimensions are not mandatory gates. A catalyst is useful but not mandatory, and a favorable technical setup is not required for a valid investment thesis. Constructs such as Quality + Valuation/Mispricing + Catalyst + Setup organize analysis rather than require every element.

---

## 2.3 Cheap ≠ undervalued

A low multiple does not automatically imply opportunity.

A company may be cheap because:

- earnings are at a cyclical peak;
- margins are unsustainable;
- the industry is structurally declining;
- debt is too high;
- competitive advantage is eroding;
- cash conversion is weak;
- the market correctly anticipates deterioration.

The core question is:

> Is the market price lower than a reasonable estimate of economic value because the market is wrong, or because the business is getting worse?

---

## 2.4 Price moves matter only relative to value

A stock falling 25% is not automatically attractive.

A stock rising 25% is not automatically expensive.

We care about:

**Price change vs change in intrinsic economic value.**

A dislocation exists when the market reaction appears materially larger than the fundamental change.

---

## 2.5 We prefer ranges over false precision

Fair value is not a single exact number.

Use:

- reasonable valuation ranges;
- scenario ranges;
- bear / base / bull cases;
- reasonable assumptions;
- explicit uncertainty.

Avoid pretending that a spreadsheet can eliminate uncertainty.

Use "reasonable valuation range" as the preferred term. "Fair value" is shorthand for an uncertain estimate, not an exact intrinsic value.

# 3. Portfolio Architecture

## 3.1 Default structure: Core / Satellite

The methodology allows both passive and selective investing.

### Core

Designed for:

- broad diversification;
- low maintenance;
- long-term compounding;
- regular contributions.

Possible instruments:

- broad-market ETFs;
- index funds;
- bonds where appropriate;
- strategic cash allocation.

Cash and bonds may be valid Core components. Their inclusion does not expand the teaching depth authorized by the [Source of Truth](../00_project/source_of_truth.md#5-program-scope).

### Satellite

Designed for:

- individual stocks;
- sector opportunities;
- market dislocations;
- higher-conviction ideas;
- temporary mispricing.

The methodology does not impose universal percentages.

Allocation depends on:

- time available;
- experience;
- risk capacity;
- risk tolerance;
- portfolio size;
- conviction;
- ability to monitor positions.

The [Investor Profile](../03_templates/templates_and_rubrics.md#6-template-000--investor-profile) supplies horizon, liquidity needs, risk capacity, risk tolerance, investable capital, and personal constraints. These inputs constrain portfolio construction, acceptable investment risk, and position sizing. Sections 12 and 13 apply those constraints without universal percentages.

---

## 3.2 Why Core / Satellite fits the target student

The target student should not depend on finding a new stock idea every month.

Core exposure allows wealth creation to continue even when no attractive individual opportunity is available.

Satellite capital preserves the ability to exploit:

- undervaluation;
- industry cycles;
- temporary fear;
- company-specific dislocations;
- attractive entry points.

# 4. Investment Decision Funnel

Every individual-stock opportunity should move through the same funnel.

## Stage 1 — Understand

Can we explain:

- what the company sells;
- who pays;
- how revenue is generated;
- what drives margins;
- where the company sits in its industry;
- what the supply chain looks like;
- what the key risks are?

If no, stop.

---

## Stage 2 — Assess Business Quality

Evaluate:

- growth quality;
- margins;
- cash generation;
- capital intensity;
- balance-sheet strength;
- dilution;
- competitive advantage;
- management capital allocation;
- industry structure.

If quality is weak, a very strong valuation case is required.

---

## Stage 3 — Understand Expectations

Ask:

- what does consensus expect?
- what does guidance imply?
- what growth appears priced in?
- what margin assumptions appear priced in?
- what is the current market narrative?

The market price reflects expectations, not only current fundamentals.

---

## Stage 4 — Value the Business

Use the simplest valuation method appropriate for the business.

Potential tools:

- P/E;
- Forward P/E;
- EV/EBITDA;
- Price / FCF;
- FCF yield;
- historical valuation;
- peer valuation;
- scenario analysis.

Use DCF only when it materially improves the decision.

Do not use complexity for its own sake.

---

## Stage 5 — Identify Mispricing or Dislocation

Ask:

- why is the market price where it is?
- what changed?
- is the issue temporary or structural?
- is the market extrapolating too much?
- are investors reacting to short-term noise?
- is the valuation discount justified?

---

## Stage 6 — Identify Catalysts

A catalyst is a plausible mechanism that may change economics or expectations.

Examples:

- earnings recovery;
- margin normalization;
- inventory normalization;
- cycle recovery;
- new product;
- capacity expansion;
- new contract;
- regulatory approval;
- deleveraging;
- buyback;
- restructuring.

A catalyst should be:

- observable;
- relevant;
- plausible;
- time-aware.

---

## Stage 7 — Evaluate Risk

Identify:

- thesis risk;
- financial risk;
- valuation risk;
- industry risk;
- customer risk;
- supplier risk;
- regulatory risk;
- execution risk;
- portfolio concentration risk.

---

## Stage 8 — Decide

Research dispositions:

**PASS:** Do not pursue the opportunity further on current evidence.

**WATCH:** Keep the opportunity under observation while entry or evidence is insufficient.

**RESEARCH MORE:** Resolve material questions before reaching an investment conclusion.

**BUY CANDIDATE:** A research conclusion indicating that the opportunity may meet investment requirements but has not yet become an executed portfolio action.

Portfolio/investment actions are **BUY / HOLD / ADD / TRIM / SELL**, governed by section 14. Research dispositions are not trade instructions. A watchlist is a tool containing research candidates, not a separate disposition.

Thesis status is **IMPROVING / UNCHANGED / DETERIORATING / BROKEN**. It describes how evidence changes an existing thesis, independently of a research disposition or portfolio action. WATCH is not a thesis status.

---

# 5. Rapid Business Understanding

The target student must be able to understand a business quickly.

The goal is not complete due diligence.

The goal is to build a sufficiently accurate first map in approximately 30–60 minutes.

Use seven blocks.

1. Business Model
2. Industry
3. Value Chain / Supply Chain
4. Economics
5. Competitive Position
6. Key Industry KPIs
7. Risk

---

## 5.1 Business Model

Understand:

- product / service;
- customer;
- revenue model;
- segments;
- geography;
- pricing;
- recurrence;
- volume;
- mix.

Core question:

> How does money enter the business?

---

## 5.2 Industry Map

Understand:

- market structure;
- major players;
- growth;
- cyclicality;
- barriers to entry;
- substitutes;
- regulation;
- competitive intensity.

Core question:

> What industry forces explain the economics of this company?

---

## 5.3 Supply Chain / Value Chain

**Value chain:** Where economic value is created and captured across the ecosystem.

**Supply chain:** The operational flow of inputs, suppliers, production, distribution, and customers.

The two may be analyzed together, but they are not identical concepts. The following map shows the operational supply chain.

Map:

**Inputs → Suppliers → Production → Distribution → Customer**

Identify:

- critical suppliers;
- bottlenecks;
- geographic dependencies;
- commodity exposure;
- inventory dynamics;
- customer concentration;
- pricing power;
- switching costs.

Core question:

> Where is value created, and where can the chain break?

---

## 5.4 Economics

Minimum financial lens:

- revenue;
- gross margin;
- operating margin;
- EPS;
- free cash flow;
- CapEx;
- debt / net debt;
- share count;
- ROIC when useful.

Core question:

> What are the few numbers that explain the machine?

---

## 5.5 Competitive Position

Competitive advantage is the formal term. "Moat" is shorthand for a durable competitive advantage with an economic consequence.

Potential sources of advantage:

- brand;
- cost;
- scale;
- network effects;
- switching costs;
- distribution;
- intellectual property;
- ecosystem;
- regulation;
- data.

A moat should have an economic consequence.

Examples:

- better margins;
- retention;
- lower acquisition cost;
- higher pricing;
- better capital returns.

---

## 5.6 Key Industry KPIs

Use a universal framework first, then sector-specific metrics.

Examples:

### SaaS
- ARR;
- NRR;
- churn;
- gross margin;
- CAC.

### Semiconductors
- ASP;
- inventory;
- utilization;
- capex;
- cycle indicators.

### Retail
- same-store sales;
- inventory;
- gross margin;
- store growth.

### Banks
- NIM;
- CET1;
- NPLs;
- loan growth.

### REITs
- FFO / AFFO;
- occupancy;
- rent growth;
- debt maturity.

### Industrials
- backlog;
- orders;
- utilization;
- input costs;
- pricing.

Rule:

> Learn the 3–5 metrics that explain the industry. Do not memorize every available metric.

---

## 5.7 Risk

Identify the main vulnerabilities and what could invalidate the business understanding using the existing [Risk Framework](#13-risk-framework). This is the seventh block, not a separate risk methodology.

# 6. Business Quality Framework

Business quality is not a single score.

Assess multiple dimensions.

## Growth

- organic vs acquired;
- durable vs temporary;
- diversified vs concentrated.

## Margins

- stable;
- improving;
- deteriorating;
- cyclical.

## Cash Conversion

Does accounting profit become cash?

## Capital Intensity

How much capital is required to maintain or grow the business?

## Balance Sheet

Can the company survive a bad operating environment?

## Returns on Capital

Does reinvestment create value?

## Shareholder Dilution

Is growth being financed by repeated equity issuance?

## Competitive Advantage

Does the business possess durable economic advantages?

## Capital Allocation

How does management use:

- retained earnings;
- CapEx;
- acquisitions;
- dividends;
- buybacks;
- debt?

---

# 7. Valuation Framework

Use the simplest tool that captures the economics.

## 7.1 Relative Valuation

Potential metrics:

- P/E;
- Forward P/E;
- EV/EBITDA;
- Price / FCF;
- FCF yield;
- Price / Sales when appropriate.

Never interpret a multiple without context.

Compare against:

- growth;
- margins;
- quality;
- leverage;
- cyclicality;
- capital intensity.

---

## 7.2 Historical Valuation

Ask:

- what range has the market historically paid?
- were growth and margins similar?
- was the interest-rate environment similar?
- has the industry structurally changed?

Historical average is context, not fair value.

---

## 7.3 Peer Valuation

Compare businesses with similar economics.

Do not compare companies merely because they share a sector label.

---

## 7.4 Scenario Valuation

Use:

- Bear;
- Base;
- Bull.

Change only the few variables that materially drive value.

Possible variables:

- revenue growth;
- margin;
- EPS;
- FCF;
- terminal multiple;
- cycle normalization.

---

## 7.5 Reverse Expectations

Ask:

> What does the current price require the company to achieve?

This is especially useful for:

- high-growth companies;
- cyclicals;
- turnaround situations;
- companies with extreme multiples.

---

## 7.6 Margin of Safety

Margin of safety is the valuation cushion between the current price and a reasonable conservative valuation range, considered together with uncertainty, downside risk, and the quality of assumptions.

It is a buffer, not a guarantee. Greater uncertainty generally requires a larger cushion. No universal fixed percentage is prescribed.

---

# 8. Dislocation Framework

A dislocation exists when:

**Price reaction > reasonable change in economic value**

Potential sources:

- earnings miss;
- guidance reduction;
- margin pressure;
- inventory correction;
- regulatory headline;
- sector selloff;
- macro shock;
- forced selling;
- operational issue.

---

## 8.1 Temporary vs Structural

### Temporary

Examples:

- inventory correction;
- short-lived input-cost pressure;
- delayed contract;
- transitory FX effect;
- one-off operational issue.

### Structural

Examples:

- permanent share loss;
- technological obsolescence;
- broken balance sheet;
- loss of moat;
- customer migration;
- regulatory impairment.

A dislocation is attractive only when:

**fundamental damage appears smaller than price damage.**

---

# 9. Catalyst Framework

A catalyst is useful but not mandatory for an investment. Analysis may explicitly conclude: "No identifiable catalyst is required." Do not invent a catalyst to complete a framework or form.

Potential catalysts:

- revenue acceleration;
- margin normalization;
- cycle recovery;
- inventory normalization;
- product launch;
- new customer;
- regulatory approval;
- debt reduction;
- buyback;
- asset sale;
- restructuring.

For each catalyst define:

- what must happen;
- evidence to monitor;
- expected timing;
- what could prevent it;
- which financial variable it affects.

---

# 10. Technical Context

Technical analysis is secondary.

A favorable technical setup is not required for an investment thesis to be valid. Apply technical context only when it improves timing or risk understanding.

It is used to improve:

- entry timing;
- risk context;
- understanding of price behavior.

Minimum framework:

- trend;
- support / resistance;
- volume;
- 50-day moving average (50DMA);
- 200-day moving average (200DMA);
- RSI.

Priority setups:

- pullback in an uptrend;
- opportunity near 200DMA;
- constructive base;
- post-earnings dislocation.

Rule:

> Technical evidence can improve timing. It cannot repair a broken fundamental thesis.

---

# 11. Entry Framework

Use entry zones rather than exact prices.

Define:

- ideal zone;
- acceptable zone;
- no-chase level.

Potential execution:

- full entry;
- initial position;
- scale-in.

Scaling in is appropriate only when:

- thesis remains intact;
- valuation remains attractive;
- risk is understood.

Averaging down without thesis review is prohibited.

---

# 12. Position Sizing

No universal percentage should be presented as correct.

The Investor Profile constraints in section 3.1 apply before selecting a position size. Within those constraints, sizing depends on:

- conviction;
- uncertainty;
- downside;
- liquidity;
- portfolio concentration;
- business quality;
- event risk;
- correlation.

Core principle:

> The greater the uncertainty, the smaller the initial position.

Define:

- starting position;
- full position;
- maximum position;
- sector limit.

---

# 13. Risk Framework

Apply the Investor Profile constraints in section 3.1 when deciding whether risk is acceptable.

Every investment must answer:

## What can go wrong?

## How much could I lose?

## What would invalidate the thesis?

## Is the portfolio already exposed to the same risk?

Risk categories:

- business;
- balance sheet;
- valuation;
- sector;
- supply chain;
- customer;
- supplier;
- regulation;
- management execution;
- portfolio concentration.

---

# 14. Buy / Hold / Add / Trim / Sell

## BUY

BUY is a portfolio action after entry, sizing, and risk considerations are accepted. It is distinct from the BUY CANDIDATE research conclusion.

Use when:

- business is understood;
- quality is sufficient;
- valuation is attractive;
- risk is acceptable;
- expected return is compelling.

## HOLD

Use when:

- thesis remains intact;
- valuation is acceptable;
- no better action is required.

## ADD

Use when:

- thesis remains intact or improves;
- expected return remains attractive;
- position size remains appropriate.

## TRIM

Use when:

- position becomes too large;
- valuation becomes stretched;
- risk rises;
- portfolio concentration becomes excessive.

Excessive position size by itself maps to TRIM.

## SELL

SELL is reserved for the conditions below, rather than excessive size alone.

Use when:

- thesis breaks;
- competitive advantage deteriorates;
- structural industry change occurs;
- financial quality deteriorates materially;
- valuation becomes extreme;
- expected return becomes unattractive;
- catalyst is realized and upside compresses;
- a clearly superior opportunity exists.

Do not sell solely because:

- price fell;
- market volatility increased;
- a negative headline appeared;
- the position is profitable.

# 15. Monitoring Framework

The target student should not watch markets continuously.

Monitoring should be:

- scheduled;
- thesis-driven;
- event-driven;
- alert-driven.

---

## 15.1 Core Positions

Review infrequently.

Examples:

- quarterly;
- semiannual;
- annual allocation review.

---

## 15.2 Individual Stocks

Review around:

- earnings;
- guidance changes;
- major news;
- valuation thresholds;
- catalyst events;
- thesis changes.

---

## 15.3 Monthly Review

Use the [Monthly Portfolio Review](../03_templates/templates_and_rubrics.md#16-template-501c--monthly-portfolio-review) to implement the monitoring principles below within the ordinary review budget in the Source of Truth. This overview describes monitoring priorities, not a separate form schema.

### Portfolio Health
- allocation;
- cash;
- concentration;
- performance.

### Existing Positions
- thesis status;
- earnings;
- risks;
- valuation.

### Watchlist
- dislocations;
- alerts;
- upcoming catalysts.

### Decisions
- buy;
- hold;
- add;
- trim;
- sell;
- rebalance;
- no action.

# 16. AI-Augmented Investing

AI is not a separate methodology.

It is embedded across the entire investment process.

Core principle:

> AI does the heavy lifting. The investor makes the decision.

---

# 17. What AI Should Do

Use ChatGPT, Claude, or equivalent tools to:

- explain concepts;
- summarize filings;
- extract structured data;
- compare companies;
- map industries;
- map supply chains;
- identify KPIs;
- compare earnings periods;
- summarize transcripts;
- build scenario tables;
- identify contradictions;
- surface risks;
- challenge a thesis;
- prioritize research;
- update existing investment memos.

---

# 18. What AI Should Not Do

Do not use AI as:

- a source of truth;
- an automatic stock picker;
- a substitute for primary sources;
- a replacement for judgment;
- a justification engine for an existing bias.

Avoid prompts such as:

> Should I buy this stock?

Prefer:

> What do I still need to understand before making this decision?

# 22. Time-Efficiency Principle

The methodology should reduce research time through staged depth. The [Source of Truth time constraint](../00_project/source_of_truth.md#22-time-constraint) governs stage budgets, additional opportunity-analysis time, and ordinary monthly review time.

## Level 1 — Rapid Screen

Question:

> Is this worth investigating?

## Level 2 — Business Understanding

Question:

> Do I understand how this business and industry work?

## Level 3 — Opportunity Analysis
This is additional research time when justified, not the total time for all stages.

Question:

> Is the valuation/dislocation attractive enough to invest?

## Level 4 — Ongoing Monitoring
Event-driven, alongside scheduled portfolio reviews.

Question:

> Has anything material changed?

Not every company deserves Level 3 analysis.

---

# 23. Research Escalation Rule

Only deepen research when the potential payoff justifies the time.

Escalate when:

- business quality is high;
- valuation gap is meaningful;
- dislocation is large;
- uncertainty is resolvable;
- catalyst is identifiable;
- position could become material.

An identifiable catalyst is not required to deepen otherwise justified research.

Stop research when:

- business cannot be understood;
- risk is unacceptable;
- valuation is clearly unattractive;
- information quality is too poor;
- thesis depends on too many assumptions.

---

# 24. Bias Control

AI and the methodology should actively challenge:

- confirmation bias;
- anchoring;
- recency bias;
- loss aversion;
- narrative fallacy;
- overconfidence;
- price anchoring;
- sunk-cost thinking.

Standard red-team question:

> What is the strongest evidence that my thesis is wrong?

---

# 25. What We Explicitly Do Not Promote

The core program does not promote:

- day trading;
- constant market monitoring;
- excessive leverage;
- options as a default strategy;
- prediction-heavy macro trading;
- blind technical analysis;
- blind value investing;
- blind passive investing;
- stock tips without thesis;
- buying because a stock fell;
- selling because a stock rose;
- DCF complexity without decision value;
- AI-generated investment decisions without verification.

---

# 26. Methodology Output

For every serious individual-stock idea, analysis should connect business understanding, economics, quality, expectations, valuation, dislocation, catalysts when useful, risk, entry, and sizing to a justified decision.

Use the canonical [Investment Decision Memo](../03_templates/templates_and_rubrics.md#22-investment-decision-memo--compact-master-template) to capture that analysis. Its fields are owned by Templates; decision meanings are owned by sections 4 and 14 of this methodology.

# 27. North Star

The methodology should make the student capable of saying:

> I understand what I own, why I own it, what the market appears to expect, what could make me wrong, and what I will do if the facts change.

That is the standard for investing with judgment.
